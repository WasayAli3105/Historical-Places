HINDU KUSH HIMALAYAS

The Hindu Kush Himalayas, often referred to as the "Roof of the World," is a vast mountain range spanning over 3,500 kilometers across Afghanistan, Pakistan, India, Nepal, Bhutan, and China. This majestic range is home to some of the highest peaks on Earth, including Mount Everest and K2, as well as a rich tapestry of cultures, languages, and biodiversity. The Hindu Kush Himalayas are not only a geographical marvel but also a vital lifeline for millions of people living in the region. The range serves as the source of many major rivers, including the Ganges, Indus, Brahmaputra, and Yangtze, which sustain the livelihoods of billions of people downstream. These rivers provide water for agriculture, hydropower generation, and domestic use, making them indispensable for the region's socio-economic development.

INDUS VALLEY CIVILISATION

The Indus Valley Civilization, also known as the Harappan Civilization, flourished from around 3300 BCE to 1300 BCE in the fertile plains of the Indus River basin, encompassing present-day Pakistan, northwest India, and eastern Afghanistan. It is one of the world's earliest urban civilizations, characterized by its advanced urban planning, sophisticated drainage systems, and intricate brickwork. The civilization's major centers, including Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro, boasted well-planned cities with grid-like street layouts, multi-story houses made of baked brick, and impressive public buildings. The cities were fortified and had sophisticated drainage and sanitation systems, indicating a high level of engineering and urban management. The people of the Indus Valley Civilization engaged in agriculture, with evidence of cultivated crops such as wheat, barley, and cotton.
